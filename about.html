About & methodology

Where the numbers come from.

ResaleWise SG turns Singapore’s public HDB resale records into something easier to read. Here is exactly what data we use, how we calculate the “value” labels, and what they can and can’t tell you.

The data

Every price on this site comes from Resale Flat Prices, published by the Housing & Development Board (HDB) on Singapore’s open-data portal, data.gov.sg. It records resale transactions of HDB flats: the town, flat type, block and street, storey range, floor area, flat model, lease start year, remaining lease and the resale price.

HDB publishes the records as several files. The older ones (1990 to February 2012) are based on the approval date of the resale application, while the newer ones (March 2012 onward) are based on the registration date. We treat both as the “month” of the sale, so the timing of the oldest records is slightly different in meaning. The data is released under the Singapore Open Data Licence; please check data.gov.sg for the current terms.

We show “transactions up to” the latest month in our database. That is the newest record we have loaded, not necessarily the newest one HDB has published. The database is refreshed by re-running our import script on a newer HDB file.

How we judge “value”

HDB prices have risen a lot over the decades, so a flat sold in 1995 will always look “cheap” next to one sold today. Comparing across years would be meaningless. So every sale is judged only against sales from its own period. The comparison point is the median (the middle price: half of comparable flats sold for less, half for more). We use the median rather than the average because a few very expensive flats would otherwise pull the average up.

What a sale is compared with

For each sale we look for the median price of the same flat type, in the same town, in the same quarter (a three-month period). We need at least 5 sales to trust a median. If the quarter is too thin we widen the comparison, one step at a time, and always tell you which one was used:

StepCompared withExample label
1Same town and flat type, same quarterTampines 4 Room, Q3 2026
2Same town and flat type, same calendar yearBedok 4 Room, 1995
3Same flat type in all towns, same quarter4 Room in all towns, Q2 1998

If even step 3 has fewer than 5 sales, we show no value label rather than guess.

On the search page, each result shows how far its price is from that comparison median, and a label:

Because each result is judged against its own period, the tags do not change when you change a filter, and old and new sales are treated alike. Hover over a tag to see exactly which sales it was compared with.

The value score on your shortlist

On the compare page, each saved flat also gets a 0 to 100 score, using the same comparison median and a bonus or penalty for remaining lease relative to other flats sold in that period (the median remaining lease of the same comparison group):

price_diff_pct = (price − period_median) ÷ period_median × 100
lease_adjustment = clamp((remaining_lease_years − peer_median_lease_years) × 0.4, −12, +12)
score = clamp(50 − 1.6 × price_diff_pct + lease_adjustment, 0, 100)

A flat priced exactly at its period’s median, with a typical lease for its peers, scores 50. Every 1% below the median adds 1.6 points; every 1% above subtracts 1.6. Having more lease left than its peers adds up to 12 points; less takes up to 12 away. The score is only shown when there is a comparison median. Because it is relative to the period, it lets you compare a 2019 sale and a 2026 sale on equal terms.

The charts on the search page (price histogram and the scatter plot) simply describe the sales you filtered. If your results span many years, their prices are not comparable with each other; use each flat’s tag for that.

What drives a price?

Each transaction page compares the sale with similar flats sold the same year: the same flat type, the same 10 sqm size band and the same 10-year band of remaining lease. Below that it lists features that go with higher prices across the whole market, each estimated the same way:

  1. Remove time and town. A sale’s price per sqm is divided by the median for its own town, flat type and quarter, so 1.00 means “typical for then and there”.
  2. Compare like with like. High and low floors are compared inside the same block; more or less remaining lease inside the same town, flat type and size band; distance to MRT inside the same town, flat type, size band and lease band. A group counts only with enough sales on both sides (10 per side within a block, 30 otherwise).
  3. Say how sure we are. The 95% range comes from resampling the matched groups 2,000 times (a bootstrap). We also show the unmatched gap, so you can see how much the matching changes the answer.

These are associations, not causes. A high floor or a short walk to the MRT tends to come with other things the records don’t include, such as views, renovation or being next to a mall. The numbers describe past sales; they don’t say what changing one feature would do to a price.

What’s nearby

For every block we measure the straight-line distance to the nearest MRT/LRT station, park, hawker centre, gym, childcare centre, primary school and secondary school, and count how many are within 500 m, 1 km and 2 km. Each kind gets a closeness score from 0 to 100: 100 when the nearest one is very close, falling evenly to 0 at a longer distance (for primary schools, 1 km and 2 km, the distances used in MOE’s Primary 1 registration). Walking time assumes about 80 m a minute with a 30% detour.

Separately, a gradient-boosted model is trained on older sales to predict each sale’s price relative to the market, and tested on the most recent months it has never seen. The “location” figure on a transaction page is how far above or below a typical block the model puts this block’s surroundings, holding flat features fixed.

Associations, not causes. Closeness is not quality, and a park or school next door is a convenience for one buyer and noise for another. Locations are as of each file’s last update, not the map on the sale date (the gym list was last updated between 2016 and 2018 and the childcare list in December 2021, so newer places are missing), and park distances are measured to the boundary to within about 50 m.

Two databases, one site

Transactions, blocks and accounts live in PostgreSQL, where joins, constraints and window functions do the work. Station locations, amenities and block points live in MongoDB, where flexible documents and geospatial indexes fit better. What this is not

  • Not a valuation or financial advice. The records don’t include renovation condition, view, noise, exact floor, or whether a sale was between relatives. Two flats with the same numbers can be worth very different amounts.
  • Not current listings. These are completed past sales, not flats for sale today.
  • Small samples are noisy. A median based on a handful of sales can move a lot. We hide town comparisons that have fewer than 5 transactions.
  • The comparison is simple. It adjusts only for town, flat type, period and remaining lease. Size, floor, view, renovation and exact location also affect price and are not included.

Questions or corrections

This is a student project (INF2003 Database Systems). If you spot a number that looks wrong, compare it with the original dataset on data.gov.sg first, as that is the source of truth.
